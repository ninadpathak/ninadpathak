# Founder essays and reflective technical prose

<!-- check_prose: word-mentions-allowed (quotes lines from rejected drafts) -->

Explain something you understand to a capable peer, in the order you would say it aloud. Give them an idea they can use to understand something they couldn't quite explain before.

PG looks closely at something, finds a problem with the apparent explanation, and works until he has a better one. His plainness helps him do that work: an ordinary sentence makes a missing reason harder to hide, and the reader can see what supports the idea and where it might fail. Removing fancy words is only part of it. A correct account of familiar behaviour can still waste the reader's time, and a clever conclusion can still be unearned. Work out the connection, then make it sound like someone who knows the subject talking.

## Before drafting

Privately answer these five questions in a few lines:

1. What do I actually know? Separate supplied experience, checked facts, deductions and missing evidence. Never turn a plausible story into the author's experience.
2. What does a daily practitioner already know? Name the actions and concepts you can assume. Identify the particular connection, consequence or unresolved question that earns their attention.
3. What makes that connection true? Trace the relevant actions in ordinary language. Change one condition and check whether your explanation predicts the result. If you cannot, research or narrow the claim.
4. In what order would I explain this aloud to that colleague? Start at the nearest shared fact needed for the inference. Decide what the introduction must establish and what the body still has to investigate.
5. What is the idea the reader does not yet have, and what did I observe that earns it? A provisional answer is enough to start; revise it as you write. If you have only a question, identify the observation that makes it worth pursuing.

Use the questions to begin thinking, not to freeze an outline. In *How to Do Great Work*, "A really good question is a partial discovery." Before delivering, check that the writing has added something: a distinction, an explanation, a useful qualification, or a question made precise enough to investigate. Do not print the protocol unless asked. Quality in one shot means doing the thinking and revision before delivery, not publishing the first draft.

## Start where the reader is

A senior engineer already knows that Claude Code uses tools and carries context between requests. Don't spend an opening paragraph explaining those facts, even accurately. Start with a decision or difficulty inside that work whose explanation deserves attention. A shared fact may be needed along the way; give it only the space its role in the inference requires. First principles means finding the operative cause of this problem, not walking a practitioner back through the definition of their tool.

Use the objects and actions someone doing the work would mention: what a failed test ruled out, what an agreement promised, what a comparison leaves unexplained. "Context economics" and "trust infrastructure" don't supply those relationships. A technical word is fine when it is the word the colleague would use. Authority comes from the distinctions you can make and the objections you can answer; an expert's discovery must be more substantial than learning that the application does its ordinary job.

## How to hook the reader

Choose an entry that supplies evidence or helps the reader recognise the problem. These are ways of judging an opening, not paragraph recipes; PG's variety matters as much as any favourite device. The earlier 106-essay classification of openings and thesis positions is in [essay-research/thinking-notes.md](essay-research/thinking-notes.md); it argues against requiring either a story or an immediate thesis.

### A lived experience with something at stake

*Beating the Averages* begins with the actual Viaweb project. The server-based product and the team's constraints later explain why their language choice mattered. "We hoped that would save us" makes clear how uncertain the choice felt at the time.

Use a real event when it contains the constraint, decision or discrepancy the essay needs. Keep the details that bear on that point. A date earns space when the historical conditions matter; it is not a badge of authenticity. A costly mistake can establish why the author cares without proving the cause of everyone else's mistakes.

**How it becomes a tic:** inventing a bill, a customer, a past belief or a moment of discovery to fill a story-shaped opening. A novice's diary makes ordinary behaviour surprising to establish an arc; a practitioner's account identifies what was hard to judge even with experience. If no personal event is supplied, use the circumstances of the work or a clearly hypothetical case.

### A story that changes what the reader sees

In *A Project of One's Own*, a son's eagerness to continue writing gives PG a recognisable experience to examine, then he corrects his own description: "I wouldn't say exactly that you're happier." In *The Airbnbs*, the failed fundraising and first hosting experience explain why the founders persisted. The story carries reasons.

Use a scene when its details change the explanation. Let a person want something, act under a constraint, and encounter a result the essay can examine. The reader should recognise the situation before being asked to accept an abstraction. A shared predicament connects as well as autobiography.

**How it becomes a tic:** scenic weather, dialogue invented for warmth, a childhood detour, or a suspenseful incident whose only purpose is delaying the point. Ask what inference disappears if you remove the story. If none does, shorten it or begin directly.

### The end of the story, told first

Open on the striking terminal moment the piece builds toward, shown plainly, then let the body walk back and supply the mechanism that makes it make sense. Nothing is withheld; this is not a curiosity hook. Pair it with the universal-outcome test: the personal incident is the evidence, and the reader finishes holding a thing they can now do, avoid or detect, not an impression of the author. Ninad rejected a set of drafts as "all about what I did and what I benefited".

### A fact, discrepancy or direct claim

*How People Get Rich Now* starts with a changed list of fortunes and asks what could explain the change. *Write Simply* starts by naming his actual practice. Neither needs a staged revelation.

Use this entry when a concrete observation or a claim already earns attention. For experts, the missing link may be between two things they know separately, or something they know in practice but haven't put into words. Supply enough context to make that link intelligible. Don't announce that you have found a surprising truth.

**How it becomes a tic:** "everyone believes," a decorative statistic, or a question whose obvious answer permits a speech. Don't require the reader to adopt a foolish belief so you can correct it.

## How to think the idea through

Choose the move that exposes a real hole in your account. You may need several, one, or none explicitly on the page. Questions are useful in private thinking; they need not become a rhetorical question-and-answer routine in the prose.

### Ask what would produce the observation

In *What I've Learned from Users*, a batch that grows from 60 to 80 startups breaks the advising process. "It was only a third more." Each partner had to know each startup, so increasing both groups multiplied the work. The number becomes informative when he finds what produces it.

When a figure or outcome interests you, find the acts, constraints or incentives that could generate it. Separate the observed result from the proposed cause. An arithmetic relation can be established more firmly than a social explanation.

**Failure mode:** stopping at the statistic, or inventing a ratio that only renames the subject. A formula comes after its terms have real meaning. Don't infer a bill from window size and elapsed time.

### Find where the current explanation breaks

In *How to Do Great Work*, "When you fix a broken model, new ideas become obvious." *Beating the Averages* shows what that involves: a programmer recognises features missing from weaker languages but may dismiss unfamiliar capabilities as unnecessary. "I thought in Basic" includes PG himself in the limitation.

Take the observation your explanation handles badly and stay with it. Try changing a premise, then see whether the other observations still fit. Explain the repaired connection so the reader can test another case.

**Failure mode:** manufacturing a conventional wisdom, declaring it wrong, and giving the same facts a new label. A useful correction must alter what the explanation predicts or lets the reader judge. Contrarian tone is not evidence of a better model.

### Supply the example that could defeat you

In *Billionaires Build*, PG describes an ideal early startup, then admits Airbnb lacked part of it: "If Airbnb didn't hit it, it must be too high." The criterion changes to what the founders understood about users. In *Having Kids*, "I only noticed them when they made noise" identifies a bias in his earlier view.

Test the strongest plausible exception, including your own experience. Let it narrow, repair or overturn the claim. Know what would make you change your mind even when the test stays in your notes.

**Failure mode:** a foolish imaginary opponent, an obligatory "to be fair," or an objection that leaves your original claim untouched. Discarding bad arguments and hiding contrary facts are different acts.

### Follow the consequence until it changes a judgment

In *The Airbnbs*, "It also taught them about their hosts" identifies what the photography trips revealed beyond their purpose. In *Beating the Averages*, the language argument reaches a concrete place to look for competitive danger: job listings.

Once a cause is clear, ask what else it implies, and follow it while each step has support. The worthwhile endpoint might be a better comparison, a changed decision or a new thing to observe. Show the connecting step.

**Failure mode:** mandatory second-order insights, a societal pronouncement, or a remedy smuggled in as the inevitable answer. A failed contractor does not by itself establish that a certification scheme will prevent the next failure.

### Find the boundary that makes the claim worth saying

*How to Write Usefully* asks for claims "as strong as they can be made without becoming false." *Donate Unrestricted* names cases where donors may know more than recipients before recommending discretion.

Replace a vague claim with the condition under which it is true. Use "I think" when it reports judgment; be direct when the claim is established. A precise conditional can be stronger than a sweeping statement.

**Failure mode:** hedging everything to sound thoughtful, or stripping qualifications to sound confident. Mechanistic plausibility does not prove prevalence. "Can" also needs a coherent route.

### Use the sentence to improve the thought

In *Good Writing*, "what do I mean to say here?" is the question behind fixing prose that sounds wrong. Write the claim plainly enough that you can inspect it. If it sounds impressive but you cannot explain it without its special phrasing, say what actually happens. You may need a different claim, another fact, or a whole branch removed. Rebuild a passage when synonym changes leave the same empty structure.

**Failure mode:** polishing a slogan before deciding whether it is true, or acting out discovery with "I realized" and "here's the twist." The useful evidence of thinking is the corrected account. Narrate the discovery only when its actual history matters.

## Voice: a person who has something to say

In *Write Like You Talk*, specialists still use the vocabulary of their subject; they don't need ceremonial language to discuss difficult ideas. A reader can hear what matters to him, what he doubts, and what he has found annoying or useful.

Give the sentence a person, thing or action it can refer to. Use ordinary verbs. Let a qualification sit where it changes the claim. A long sentence is fine when its clauses belong together; don't break it into dramatic fragments to look plain. Keep the actual objects and actions in view: turning a failed test into "the cost of reconstructing the work" sounds analytical while hiding the meaning.

| Documentation register | The same fact said aloud |
|---|---|
| "A retained failed approach may provide exclusionary evidence during subsequent debugging." | "That failed attempt may be worth keeping if it stops Claude from trying it again." |
| "A decrease in context occupancy does not establish a corresponding decrease in billed input cost." | "You can't tell how much you saved just by counting the tokens you removed." |
| "The portfolio establishes visual appearance but not compliance with the agreement." | "The photos show how the room looks. They don't tell you whether the contractor did what he agreed to do." |
| "Airbnb's lack of growth when we met them shows that initial growth is not a necessary condition." | "Airbnb had no growth when we met them. That can't be a requirement." (Illustrative paraphrase of PG's reasoning; the first person is his.) |

Draft 11's "Once you look at what's in there, your own messages are a rounding error" has a speaking voice: someone judging the size of things. "The reread is cheap" names a transaction in a colleague's words. Borrow that directness; the claims still need evidence for the particular case. A grammatical sentence can remain lifeless: "Your next message arrives with the work that led up to it" sounds written, and "The next request includes those earlier test results" is clear but still needs a reason to be here. A voice fix and a reader-level fix are separate jobs.

First person can state a present judgment without inventing a past event. "I'd want to know what happened after the photos were taken" is a question the writer owns; "I called five former clients" requires five actual calls. Don't manufacture practice, emotions, dialogue or private beliefs. Preserve the author's supplied loss or frustration without performing it. Humour can come from a revealing detail (the investor who left without finishing his smoothie); do not add jokes to meet a tone requirement.

Cut announcements that postpone the thought: "That's the thing," "the real question," "here's the twist," "it turns out," "it's worth noting." Cut abstract noun piles, inflated praise and rehearsed punchlines. Don't use "X is not Y. It's Z," a colon reveal, a self-answered question or a trio of claims as a drafting engine. An actual contrast, question or set of three remains available when the subject needs it; there is no stylistic allowance to spend.

## The introduction and the body

The introduction establishes the backstory, why the subject deserves attention, and what needs explaining. Backstory may be the circumstances of doing the work; it does not require a dated memory, a dramatic discovery or a catalogue of what the tool does. Check that the hook leads into the requested subject: a context-cost article should not become a cleanup guide because you found a good opening about clearing a session.

Leave the body something to establish: evidence to examine, a cause to test, a distinction to work through. "Why" belongs in an introduction; a complete inventory of causes, rates, implementation details and remedies belongs in the body. Don't confuse depth with packing the entire argument into the opening.

End the intro where the investigation can begin, with the unresolved issue already understandable. An article plan, a promise of surprise, or a final sentence naming your new framework adds little; a list of dimensions is still an outline when introduced by "I want to understand." Don't fill a last paragraph to complete a three-paragraph shape. "I measured one session" is useful only when that measurement exists; an intention to examine something can be stated plainly. "I need to understand the problem before fixing it" describes your process; say what about the problem needs explaining instead.

In the body, follow the reader's next need: a claim its reason, the reason made concrete where necessary, a serious exception accounted for. These are dependencies, not a sequence to impose on each section. Let the subject make the transition; you don't need "Why?" between a result and its cause.

End on the last useful consequence or observation. A callback ending works when the opening now means something different. Don't force a return to a number, a polished moral or an aphorism. *The Airbnbs* closes on a documented seven-word reply whose force comes from the growth figures before it, not from the wording.

## Evidence and changing facts

Keep the narrator's standing honest. Never invent an implementation, customer, bill, experiment, date, quotation or history of expertise. A supplied event establishes what happened to that person; the explanation of it is a separate claim. A provocative title is a question to investigate, not proof of its premise. Distinguish a bad outcome, a possible route to repeating it, and evidence that it happened again. If your account depends on one person controlling information, check the independent routes by which others could learn it, and distinguish a useful source of evidence from the only possible source.

Check current product behaviour, prices, limits and versions against primary sources when tools are available. If a detail cannot be checked, omit the current-specific assertion or state what is missing outside the draft. Historical sample prose is not current documentation, and PG's claims are examples of writing to examine, not facts to adopt.

Use a mechanism when a changing number is incidental. When magnitude matters, use the actual measurement with its denominator, date and conditions. Replacing "[N]k of 200k" with "most of the window" does not fix an invented figure. Separate capacity, repeated usage and price: a cached input can occupy space while costing less to reuse; API estimates and subscription bills are different things; a lower rate does not establish that the total is cheap. Don't load an intro with those details to show you checked them.

Use brackets only for the author's unknown specifics when an unfinished draft is requested. Don't bracket generic market facts, prices or product limits. Final prose has its evidence or makes a narrower claim that stands without it.

## Final read

Read the draft as a capable colleague who can interrupt. Check the thought before the rhythm:

- What can this reader now understand or investigate that they couldn't before? Point to the observation and the step that earns it.
- Where would they say "I know that"? Remove the lesson unless a new inference depends on it here.
- Where would they say "How do you know?" Check the source, the deduction and its limits. Test a credible alternative.
- Where would they say "Nobody talks like this"? Re-explain that passage aloud in your own words; replace its phrasing and, if needed, its order.
- Does the narrator's knowledge fit their experience? Is any anecdote, surprise, action, emotion or result invented?
- Does the intro provide backstory and why/what, with real work left for the body? Does its last paragraph add a reason to continue?
- Are ordinary words concealing an empty claim, or short sentences manufacturing drama? Check the corresponding rejected draft.

Repair a failed check before delivery. No automatic pass because no banned phrase appeared. Read the full passage again after changing a rule; a local fix can leave the same failure in another form.
