# Narrative nonfiction and personal accounts

[core.md](../core.md) applies throughout. This file has two parts: researched nonfiction that develops an argument, and a first-person account of real experience.

## Researched nonfiction: essays and explainers

Write as a knowledgeable colleague with something worth telling one realistic person. Interesting prose changes what that person understands about the subject. Smooth transitions and a friendly tone cannot supply a missing idea.

### Research before writing

**Always research both the topic and its actual audience before drafting or rewriting.** The topic determines the imagined reader. Do not start with a convenient persona, draft from familiarity, or decorate an already-written argument with sources.

Use fresh authoritative primary sources for mechanisms and factual claims. Read real questions, disagreements, experiences, or interviews from the relevant audience to learn what people know, believe, and struggle with. Audience discourse supports an account of their concerns; it does not prove technical facts or establish how common an experience is.

Fresh outside research is required when accessible, even if the prompt supplies facts. Supplied material can stand alone only when external research is prohibited, unavailable, or explicitly waived. Honor a specified audience and research that person's circumstances. If evidence is insufficient, explain what is missing before drafting rather than inventing a reader.

Stop when the evidence supports a recognizable reader, the relevant mechanism, and a worthwhile observation. Use no source quotas or exhaustive search ritual. Keep compact internal provenance notes; include public citations where the claims need them, without a compulsory bibliography.

Before outlining, establish internally:

- This person's situation, existing knowledge, reasonable beliefs, stake, and live question. Infer these from research; do not invent a name, biography, or demographic profile.
- What the material reveals beyond a definition, taxonomy, or familiar recommendation. Identify the particular detail, mechanism, tension, or consequence that earns an essay.
- Which evidence changed your initial understanding or selection. If research merely confirms generic advice, investigate the unresolved question or narrow the piece. Do not manufacture novelty or pretend a familiar fact is a revelation.

Keep these notes out of the prose unless requested. Ask only when missing context materially changes the work.

### Find the inquiry before choosing the scaffolding

For an essay, identify the tension the research makes worth pursuing and the provisional position it supports. Know what would complicate that position. A sequence of accurate definitions or examples proving a framework announced in the opening is not yet a developing argument. For an explainer, follow the mechanism the reader needs; do not invent controversy to sound interesting.

Choose evidence for different jobs: establishing a situation, explaining a mechanism, testing a belief, or revealing a consequence. Let a reasonable objection or a real benefit modify the position rather than stage an objection only to dismiss it. A concession should affect what the writer can conclude. Scrutinize a proposed remedy for the failures or costs it may introduce. When exploring alternative outcomes, state their premises and the uncertainty separating them; a shared conclusion remains bounded by those assumptions.

When working toward the user's preferred essay style, read [the five Noema reference analyses](narrative-research/noema-essay-mechanics.md). They show distinct reportorial and philosophical approaches. Learn their choices without requiring anecdotes, satire, historical detours, theoretical names, or a recurring structure. These references provide craft evidence, not a fact bank for a new topic.

### Develop the thought

Begin with recognizable material that carries the actual question: a decision, an observation, a contradiction, or a concrete consequence. Choose the opening the subject earns. No compulsory anecdote, historical preface, dramatic hook, or statement of the article's framework.

Select detail that lets this colleague see what you see. Explain the mechanism behind it and follow its consequences far enough to change their understanding. A fact that only lends authority, a citation that only endorses advice, or an example followed by an obvious lesson can usually go.

Grant reasonable beliefs before examining their limits. Distinguish familiar shared knowledge from a belief worth testing and a contested premise the argument needs. Support that premise; do not smuggle it in as something everyone knows.

The reader's knowledge grows as the passage proceeds. Connect new material to an established object, inference, or unresolved question. Repair a missing causal or inferential bridge, but let the reader make inferences they can already make. Continuity does **not** mean explaining how every sentence relates to the previous one.

Use concrete referents. Introduce terms when their meaning becomes useful. Return to an established term when a later example changes its implications; do not redefine it or swap synonyms for variety. Use “but,” “so,” and “therefore” only for real relationships. Do not glue unrelated facts together with transitions or repeat a conclusion as “This means...” after the example has established it.

Let the argument choose its structure. Sections need different work, not matching templates. Paragraphs finish a thought, including its necessary qualification; they have no universal sentence count. Elaboration, accumulation, emotional emphasis and a pause over consequences can earn space without adding a new fact in every sentence. Vary pace with the material rather than alternating long explanation and punchy verdict on schedule.

A title should promise a specific concern or consequence. A subtitle can establish the interpretive stakes, qualify the title, or identify the mechanism and scope before a complex opening. End when the final concrete implication, decision, or unresolved question has earned its place. Return to an opening only when doing so adds understanding; omit automatic recaps and polished morals.

### Preserve the colleague's voice and intelligence

For a revision, identify the author's actual judgment and distinctive lines before changing anything. Keep useful vocabulary, uncertainty, bluntness, humor, and unevenness. Rebuild only what is failing; do not polish every paragraph into the same shape.

Conversational writing can be precise and intellectually demanding. It does not require direct “you,” first person, slang, rhetorical questions, or short sentences. Keep a long causal sentence when it is clear. Avoid explaining definitions this person already knows or narrating how they should interpret every detail.

Use authorial judgment grounded in the material. Never invent personal experience, data, quotations, consensus, or confidence to make prose lively. Distinguish observations, inferences, and conditional claims. Qualification should identify real uncertainty; vague hedges are no substitute for taking a supported position.

A metaphor or joke should expose a relationship or recognizable contradiction. Preserve strong existing lines rather than adding performative edge. For a load-bearing analogy, identify the shared mechanism and check where the resemblance stops; analogy is not empirical proof. Keep reporting, conceptual interpretation, causal inference and forecast distinct, even when the voice is confident.

### Edit out generic polish

Apply [editing.md](editing.md) to the draft while keeping this file's research workflow. A clean word scan does not prove the writing works, so run these checks too:

- **Contribution:** What work does this paragraph do for the colleague: establish, develop, complicate, substantiate, or make a consequence felt? Delete redundant paraphrases; preserve earned elaboration and emphasis. A paragraph need not deliver a new fact to matter.
- **Portability:** Could this sentence move unchanged into an essay about another subject? General principles can earn their place, but they need a specific mechanism, consequence, or decision. Cut generic advice and connective filler.
- **Trust:** Does the prose tell the reader that something matters, explain an already-clear distinction, or repeat the meaning of its example? Delete that commentary and see whether the passage still makes sense.
- **Rhythm:** Are sections or paragraphs repeatedly premise–example–lesson, or the same length and sentence shape? Let their actual work determine the shape. Do not replace symmetry with artificial fragments.
- **Judgment:** Is there a substantive position, with appropriate limits, or only agreeable statements nobody could dispute? Sharpen the supported decision; do not invent a provocative claim.

Treat faux-insight setups, metadiscourse, contrast formulas, synonym cycling and recap endings as places to inspect. Remove generic or redundant uses. Framing can earn its place when it identifies an unresolved question or changes the scope; a brief return can carry a changed implication. Do not turn an editorial checklist into mechanical removal of voice. Explicit wording bans remain binding. Keep a transition when its removal loses a genuine relationship; remove it when the reader can follow without help.

### Final read

Read the whole piece as the researched colleague. Check whether its specific observations reward your attention, its connections hold without continual narration, and its qualifications preserve the truth. For analysis, quote the actual failure and explain the missing substance or inference; do not guess whether AI wrote the text.

Classify the deliverable before applying technical-writing conventions. An interpretive technical essay needs accurate mechanisms and an earned position; it does not require runnable steps in every section, pass/fail checks, a classification table, or a two-sentence paragraph limit merely because its subject is technical. A tutorial may require those practical structures. Preserve real project gates and report publication incompatibilities rather than silently changing them. Explicit user instructions take precedence. This skill authorizes no unrelated edits or publication.

## Personal accounts: essays, career reflections and building stories

Write as a person explaining something that happened and why it still matters to them. The voice is warm, direct, and willing to admit uncertainty. Keep the writer's actual perspective; use the craft of the reference without borrowing its sentences, biography, or identity.

### Find the material

Use the user's notes, draft, interview answers, and relevant confirmed context. Establish the intended reader, what happened, what the writer did, and what they now make of it. Infer the format from the request; ask only when a missing answer would change the piece.

Keep events and the writer's interpretation distinct. Never invent a first-person experience, emotion, motive, remembered thought, quotation, date, number, client result, or relationship. A plausible detail is still an invention. Use indirect speech when only the substance of a conversation is supplied; quotation marks require supplied wording. Preserve uncertainty in approximate memories.

If a personal account is essential and the experience is missing, ask for the real incident and what changed. If the user wants an explanation or gives only a topic, write direct, concrete exposition; do not manufacture a narrator's past. When adding public or technical claims, research appropriate primary sources and the intended reader's actual questions. External sources can establish context, not memories on the user's behalf.

### Shape the piece around its real movement

Find a specific reason to tell this story to this reader. It may be a changed judgment, an unresolved difficulty, a small success, or gratitude toward someone. Do not require a dramatic setback, vulnerability, or a triumphant finish.

Let one event make the next understandable. Chronology works well when decisions and consequences accumulate; a brief present-day frame can work when it gives the past a purpose. Start closer to the material if a frame adds nothing. Include the ordinary task, constraint, tool, or action that lets the reader follow what changed. Compress uneventful stretches and give consequential decisions enough room.

Keep technical detail at the level needed to understand the person's work and choices. In a tutorial or other explicitly requested format, preserve its functional structure, accuracy, and necessary steps; apply the voice without turning it into a memoir.

End at the consequence the material supports: a current practice, a new question, a specific acknowledgment, or help the writer can actually offer. Encouragement must fit the experience and its limits. A single person's outcome does not prove everyone can achieve it. A return to the opening is optional and should add meaning.

### Keep the voice human and specific

- Use familiar words, concrete verbs, and natural contractions. Let enthusiasm, doubt, pride, or embarrassment be plain when the writer has expressed them.
- Sound like one person talking to another. Retain the writer's useful asides, modest humor, and characteristic vocabulary; do not add jokes, exclamations, or rhetorical questions to meet a quota.
- Let sentences and paragraphs follow the thought. Keep a longer sentence when it carries connected events clearly, and a brief one when it gives a real moment space. Avoid automatic one-line paragraphs or repeated setup–lesson patterns.
- Prefer what the writer tried, noticed, or chose over polished claims about their qualities. Keep the people who helped in view when the facts support it.
- Preserve candid uncertainty and imperfect outcomes. Remove inflated transformation language, generic inspiration, staged revelations, and lessons that merely restate the preceding event. Correct errors without sanding every passage into the same smooth voice.

### Revise and deliver

Before rewriting, identify the draft's strongest specific details and the writer's actual judgment. Preserve them while repairing confusing order, missing connections, repetition, and unsupported additions.

Revise by cutting before adding voice. Read neighboring sentences together: remove setup phrases such as "The harder question is…" when the explanation can stand directly, and cut an echoed lesson after an example. Inspect neat contrasts, announced turns, and polished aphorisms for the same empty framing or repetition. Keep connections the reader needs, and preserve concrete details, asides, judgments, and natural pauses that carry the story or personality. A sentence can earn its place through voice or emotional texture without adding a new fact. Do not flatten an account into instructions to make it shorter.

First person is not a quota. Use "I" for an action or judgment supported by the material, not to make a factual explanation sound personal. In technical pieces, let the actual code, task, decision, and result carry the voice; do not add a preference or a tidy moral to compensate for missing personal history.

Read the result for factual fidelity, connected thought, natural spoken rhythm, and a reason for this reader to care. Check every added scene detail and interior thought against the supplied material. Keep the requested length and format; default to connected prose for an essay. Deliver the piece without process commentary unless the user requests analysis or a material fact remains unresolved.

It contains a compact source analysis and an original illustration, not reusable facts about the user.
