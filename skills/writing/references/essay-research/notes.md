# Reading notes: Paul Graham essays, newest to oldest

Source: https://paulgraham.com/articles.html, 232 essays fetched 2026-09-05 into /tmp/pg-essays/txt/.
Per-essay numbers in per_essay.tsv; corpus numbers in stats.json. Format per essay:
**Title** (date) — open / first para / sentences / examples / "I" / para endings / ending / tics.

## Batch 0 (Aug 2026 – Jul 2024)

**How Universities Should Prepare Founders** (Aug 2026) — Opens with the question in the title as a literal question, 8 words. First para answers who's qualified to answer ("we get them next. We're like grad school."). Median 16 w/s. Examples: Jobs's calligraphy, Zuckerberg psych major, Harvard vs Yale application rates (a real number: "about twice"), reading period producing Microsoft and Meta. "I" rare, mostly "I would not want to draw a sharp line", "I know exactly what a class of this type should look like, because YC is it." Paras end on the sharpest restatement ("Turn a blind eye to it."). Ends with a conditional prediction, no recap. Tics: "In fact", "Indeed", "So" as paragraph openers; question-then-answer pairs; "Imagine what X would be if..."; one-line paragraph as a punch ("Right now there are students flying drones out of line of sight. Turn a blind eye to it.").

**How to Earn a Billion Dollars** (Jun 2026) — Talk. Opens by naming the audience ("future prime ministers' club") then a flat promise. First para ends on a joke that is also the thesis. Median 13. Evidence: 6500 companies, "about 30 billionaires", a founder at 93%/month, a live calculation the audience does on phones. "I" is the narrator of a specific week ("last month", "A couple days later", "Later that day"). Paragraph endings are one-clause verdicts ("And that gets you exponential growth."). Ending: an explicit summary, but he announces it as being for the politicians only, i.e. the recap is framed as a concession. Tics: "Of course it's possible. It's hard, but it's possible." (repeat with variation); rhetorical question then one-word answer ("How do you do that? By feeling the need yourself.").

**How to Convert Between Wealth and Income Tax** (May 2026) — Opens with the title as a question. Second para: politicians don't know the question exists. Third para: the answer, a number ("about 20"). Then the worked example with $100. Ends by restating the number-as-insight in a new form: "The answer's not hard to understand, once you realize the question exists." Paragraphs are 1–3 sentences. Zero anecdotes; the example is arithmetic.

**The Brand Age** (Mar 2026) — Long history essay. Opens with a dated event: "In the early 1970s, disaster struck the Swiss watch industry." First para corrects the popular name ("Now people call it the quartz crisis, but in fact it was a compound of three separate disasters"). Median 15. Examples are named and numbered: Bretton Woods .228 → .625 USD, watch sizes 33 mm vs 42 mm, brand height 0.5–0.75 mm, "8 square millimeters to 800". "I" appears late and sparingly ("I haven't talked about Rolex so far", "as someone who knew these investment bankers, I'd lean toward the audience"). Paragraph endings often a compressed aphorism: "Branding is centrifugal; design is centripetal." "The brand must not break character." Coins a term, but defines it with a mechanism ("comb-over effect: when a series of individually small changes takes you from something a little bit off to something freakishly wrong"). Ending: pivots from dark to a two-word rule ("Follow the problems.") then a final future-tense sentence. Tics: "Indeed", "In fact", "Or more precisely," self-correction mid-argument; "Well that was dark." conversational aside; questions as section pivots.

**The Shape of the Essay Field** (Jun 2025) — Opens with a definition: "An essay has to tell people something they don't already know." Then a taxonomy of three. Median 12. Example: The Selfish Gene changed his thinking. "I" enters when he reports his own case ("I noticed empirically that I seemed to be writing for the young"). Ends thinking out loud: "Interesting question. I should think about that." Tic: "(a)...(b)...(c)" enumeration in one sentence; "Or more precisely".

**Good Writing** (May 2025) — Opens with a two-part definition ("two senses in which writing can be good"), 20 words. First para ends with the claim: "I think writing that sounds good is more likely to be right." Median 14. Evidence: his own typesetting 30 years ago, lines that ran a line long. Analogies: shaking a bin of objects; plane shape vs car color (Kelly Johnson). One-word sentences as examples: "Glitter. Round. Scrape. Prim. Cavalcade." Anticipates the objection in the reader's voice: "Ok, many people will be thinking, this seems plausible so far, but what about liars?" Ends on a parallel: "It's hard to be right without sounding right." Tics: "So it is with writing."; "It's obvious once you think about it."

**What to Do** (Mar 2025) — Opens with a four-word question. First para admits the question sounds strange and defends it. Answer in para 2 in three items, the third the point. Median 13. "I" is a reasoner ("I can't prove that one should do this"). Ends with a consequence, not a summary: "you're just copying yourself."

**The Origins of Wokeness** (Jan 2025) — Opens on a word and its dictionary definition ("The word 'prig' isn't very common now"). Builds a history with dates (1982, 1986, 1988, 2010, 2020). "I" as eyewitness: "I saw political correctness arise. When I started college in 1982 it was not yet a thing." One quantified anecdote from HN: "about three times more likely to upvote something if it outraged them." Many paragraphs end on a short verdict sentence. One-sentence paragraph as a beat: "We can't get rid of the aggressively conventional-minded." Ends with a rule in two short sentences: "The number of true things we can't say should not increase. If it does, something is wrong." Tics: "Why there? And why then?"; "Obviously not;" answering his own question; "Curiously enough".

**Writes and Write-Nots** (Oct 2024) — Opens with a prediction and a hedge that is itself the hook ("I'm usually reluctant to make predictions about technology, but"). 537 words. Examples: doctors and moles, JFK paid, MLK plagiarized, professors' petty thefts, blacksmiths. Ends with an analogy carried to its last step: "There will still be smart people, but only those who choose to be." Tic: coined pair "writes and write-nots" then "thinks and think-nots".

**When To Do What You Love** (Sep 2024) — Opens by naming a debate and saying the yes/no answer is impossible; "The only way to give a general answer is to trace it." Examples: football, Bill Gates loving writing software for customers. Coins "midwit peak" and "staying upwind" only after showing the shape. Ends with the one case where the answer is simple, then "you can't manufacture that."

**Founder Mode** (Sep 2024) — Opens on a specific event last week (Brian Chesky's talk) with a concrete detail (Ron Conway forgot to take notes). Median 15. The argument comes from what founders said in the room, not from theory. Names the thing after describing the symptom ("In effect there are two different ways to run a company"). Admits ignorance as content: "We still don't know. That's how little we know about founder mode." Ends with a contrast of two names: Steve Jobs vs John Sculley.

**The Right Kind of Stubborn** (Jul 2024) — Opens with a 6-word generalization ("Successful people tend to be persistent.") then its opposite, then the question whether they differ. Median 11.5, the shortest in the batch. Evidence: "I've talked to a lot of determined people"; the Collison brothers listen "with an almost predatory intensity." Images: boats whose engines can't be throttled vs boats whose rudders can't be turned; aerobic vs anaerobic respiration. Paragraphs end on the two-sentence contrast: "The persistent are attached to the goal. The obstinate are attached to their ideas about how to reach it." Ends: "kids and drunks and fools are best at it... but when they do the results are magical."

Batch 0 pattern: 7 of 12 open on a question or a definition; 3 open on a dated event or a person. Every essay names at least one real person or number. No essay opens with a scene-setter about "today's world". Recaps appear only in the talk, and he flags it as a concession to the audience.

## Batch 1 (Mar 2024 – Nov 2022)

**The Reddits** (Mar 2024) — Opens with a personal fact, 10 words: "I met the Reddits before we even started Y Combinator." Chronological story, every para a scene with a checkable detail (nickname "Cell food muffins", del.icio.us/popular, snoo.com too expensive, launched "about three weeks into the first YC batch"). "I" is a participant, never a commentator. Ends with an open claim: "Steve is not out of ideas yet." Tic: correction of an old belief in passing ("since we thought then that we were funding ideas rather than founders").

**How to Start Google** (Mar 2024) — Talk to 14-15 year olds. Opens by naming what the audience believes and denying it ("Most of you probably think... That's not true"). Median 12.5. The sticking-door-and-carpenter image runs the whole essay and returns as a two-word refrain ("Door sticks."). Real examples told as scenes in present tense: Zuckerberg 2002, "rugby" search in 1997. Ends in a one-line recap because it's a talk to kids: "That's it, just two things, build stuff and do well in school." Tics: "(Do you notice a theme here?)"; "Can you predict what I'm going to say next?"; "There's that word again."

**The Best Essay** (Mar 2024) — Opens by disclaiming its own title. Thinks in public: "In this essay I've already cut a 17-paragraph subtree." Defines "question" in his own broad sense before using it. Examples: Darwin 1844, "How can they all be finches?", traders' "edge", Calder mobile, Robert Morris vs "20 new smart people". Ends on a question, the essay's true subject: "How to get more questions? That is the most important question of all." Tics: "Or more precisely"; "Indeed"; "Ditto"; a section return announced ("the Cubist trick of laying separate subtrees end to end").

**Superlinear Returns** (Oct 2023) — Opens with a childhood misunderstanding, 28 words. Second para quotes the cliché he's attacking ("You get out what you put in") and refutes it with a mechanism in two sentences ("You get no customers, and you go out of business."). Reduces the topic to two causes, names them, then tests each. One-sentence paragraph as a reaction: "Even after decades of thinking about this, I find that sentence startling." Gives a "recipe in one paragraph" as a run of imperatives. Ends on a physical image: "it may grow into a mountain beneath you."

**How to Do Great Work** (Jul 2023) — 10.4k words, 202 paragraphs, median 14 w/s; the paragraphs are short (2-4 sentences) and mostly imperatives. Opens with a method question and "I decided to find out by making it." Aphorism paragraphs of one sentence: "Style is doing things in a distinctive way without trying to. Trying to is affectation." "Be professionally curious about a few topics and idly curious about many more." Examples: Einstein and Maxwell, Durer, Watt, heliocentrism, "one of the most original thinkers I know decided to focus on dating after he got divorced." Anticipates the reader: "Believe it or not, I tried to make this essay as short as I could." Ends with a question addressed to the reader: "Why not by you?"

**How to Get New Ideas** (Jan 2023) — 103 words. Opens with the answer: "The way to get new ideas is to notice anomalies". Two paragraphs, one image (fractal knowledge). No "I" at all.

**The Need to Read** (Nov 2022) — Opens on childhood sci-fi "tapes". Claim: "There is a kind of thinking that can only be done by writing." Ends on a contrast: "people who want to have ideas can't afford to."

**What You (Want to)\* Want** (Nov 2022) — Opens with "Since I was about 9 I've been puzzled by" (35 words, long for him). Method stated outright: "start with a slightly wrong version, and then fix it." Iterates the sentence, then ends with a regex. Humor is in the form itself.

Batch 1 pattern: the "wrong version, then fix it" move appears explicitly (Want), and implicitly everywhere (Superlinear, Best Essay). Talks recap; essays don't. He states the method of the essay inside the essay without apology.

## Batch 2 (Oct 2022 – Feb 2021)

**Alien Truth** (Oct 2022) — Opens with a conditional thought experiment, 17 words. Names examples immediately (math, mass of a carbon atom, Occam's razor). "(Let's see if that one does.)" — a parenthetical bet on his own claim. Ends by applying the idea to itself.

**What I've Learned from Users** (Sep 2022) — Opens with a recent event ("I recently told applicants") and a one-line quoted piece of advice set as its own paragraph. Numbers: 100 startups, "batch that broke YC" summer 2012, 60 → 80, O(n^2). One quoted partner. Three-sentence paragraph as a chant: "Speed defines startups. Focus enables speed. YC improves focus." Horror-movie image (the door ajar). Ends: "founders are surrounded by people who want to help them, and can."

**Heresy** (Apr 2022) — Opens "One of the most surprising things I've witnessed in my lifetime is". Quotes Westfall on Newton, then reports his own two readings of the passage 20 years apart. Defines with "(1)... (2)..." then tests each. Uranium-on-the-scale image. Announces what he's deliberately not doing ("I've deliberately avoided mentioning any specific heresies") and why. Measles analogy: "The problem is the derivative." repeated verbatim as a refrain. Ends optimistic, forward-looking.

**Putting Ideas into Words** (Feb 2022) — Opens with the claim itself, 21 words. "the stranger" as a personified reader. Numbers: "2 weeks on an essay and reread drafts 50 times." Self-aware: "The reason I've spent so long establishing this rather obvious point is that it leads to another that many people will find shocking." Ends: "though it's not a sufficient condition, it is a necessary one."

**Is There Such a Thing as Good Taste?** (Nov 2021) — Opens with "When I was a kid, I'd have said there wasn't. My father told me so." Announces method: proof by reductio. Personal evidence: he studied painting and saw the gap to Leonardo and Bellini. Vaccine analogy for "objective" properties. Ends with partial order: "not possible to have perfect taste, it is possible to have good taste."

**Beyond Smart** (Oct 2021) — Opens with what "most would say" about Einstein and then contradicts it in the same paragraph. "Perhaps you did too. But I bet it's not what you really want." Admits: "This is the first time I've posed the question to myself this way." Ends on a coined phrase used once: "mine this gap for discoveries about discovery."

**Weird Languages** (Aug 2021) — 329 words. Opens by reframing a common claim as a statement about the speaker. "99.5%... the other .5%" made-up-but-honest numbers. "A concrete example: Lisp macros." Ends on think/say parallel.

**How to Work Hard** (Jun 2021) — Opens by denying there's anything to learn, then "the answer is definitely yes." Quotes Gates ("Not one."), Wodehouse, Hardy, Patrick Collison (age 13 or 14). Personal specifics: stopped watching TV at 13; "My limit for the harder types of writing or programming is about five hours a day." Dog circling before lying down. Ends on "productive in a way few people are."

**A Project of One's Own** (Jun 2021) — Opens with his nine-year-old son wanting to get home to write his story (33 words). Skating vs walking runs the whole essay. His father and math problems as the example of owning assigned work. Mac team: "not tennis balls hit by Steve Jobs, but rockets let loose by Steve Jobs." Ends on the two-part ideal.

**Fierce Nerds** (May 2021) — Opens "Most people think of nerds as quiet, diffident people." then reverses it within the paragraph with the quarterback-at-a-symposium image. Watson's first sentence quoted as evidence. "7 of the 8 richest people in America are now fierce nerds." Turns to address the reader directly ("young fierce nerd"). Ends with a joke that is also a claim (singularity).

**Crazy New Ideas** (May 2021) — Opens with one fear he has. Efficient-market framing. Hatchling vs full-grown eagle. Copernicus 1532 to mid-17th century. Ends: "These people need your help. And if you help them, you'll probably learn something in the process."

**An NFT That Saves Lives** (May 2021) — Announcement. Numbers carry it: 165 hospitals, 9 per 1000, 133,733 families, $1,235. Ends: "What a sentence to be able to write."

**The Real Reason to End the Death Penalty** (Apr 2021) — Opens by listing what intellectuals talk about, then "But in practice". Eight named cases, each a short paragraph with years served. "I could go on." Ends: "in practice capital punishment means killing innocent people."

**How People Get Rich Now** (Apr 2021) — Opens with a data source and dates (Forbes, 1982 vs 2020). Every paragraph has a count. Ice-crust pond image. Reframes the question ("not why people are starting companies, but why they're starting companies again"). Ends with a rhetorical question: "how could it not be?"

**Write Simply** (Mar 2021) — Opens with a 10-word statement of practice: "I try to write using ordinary words and simple sentences." saltimbocca/saltintesta joke. Chair built to last. Ends with his process: fast first draft, days of cutting.

**Donate Unrestricted** (Mar 2021) — Opens "The secret curse of the nonprofit world is restricted donations." Two exceptions named (university, Gates Foundation). Ends with the instruction restated as trust.

**What I Worked On** (Feb 2021) — 12.5k words autobiographical. Opens with a flat inventory: "Before college the two main things I worked on, outside of school, were writing and programming." Concrete everywhere: IBM 1401, TRS-80 in about 1980, $7 a day in Florence, $100/$300 a month, 70 stores end of 1996, 500 end of 1997, options worth "$2 million a month", March 11 at Garden and Walker, Bel from March 26 2015 to October 12 2019. Lessons stated once each in one clause. Self-mockery is precise: "with my unerring nose for financial opportunity". Rtm's brain a colonial New England church, Trevor's Austrian Rococo. The last sentence names itself: "and this is the last sentence of it."

Batch 2 pattern: openings that name a belief and reverse it happen inside the first paragraph, never across several. When numbers exist he gives them raw and unrounded (133,733; $1,235). Analogies are physical objects (uranium, eagle, ice crust, chair, dog). Endings mostly restate the thesis in a fresh form or turn it on itself; none summarize the sections.

## Batch 3 (Dec 2020 – Dec 2019)

**Earnestness** (Dec 2020) — Opens with a private vocabulary ("Jessica and I have certain words"). Defines earnest with a vector image (direction and magnitude). Lists fields where earnestness doesn't matter, then notices they're the fields he'd avoid. Ends by admitting he wandered into startups again: "an example of an x nerd in the wild."

Coins "larval market" and immediately anchors it (Apple 1976). "If I had to decide whether to fund startups based on a single question, it would be 'How do you know people want this?'" Airbnb cereal story as evidence. Bracketed "⟨elaborate expletive⟩". Ends: "That's how they became billionaires."

**The Airbnbs** (Dec 2020) — Opens by stating purpose in one sentence. Quotes: "He didn't even finish his smoothie." $4000/month = $3500 rent + $500 food taped to the mirror. Weekly numbers $460, $897, $1428. Ends on a seven-word quote, and he counts the words for you.

**How to Think for Yourself** (Nov 2020) — Opens with a class of work and three examples in the first two paragraphs (scientist, investor, founder). Three components named then examined one at a time. Submarine sandwich image for ideologies. "They all know the secret handshake though." Ends with a revised motto.

**Early Work** (Oct 2020) — Opens with a fear and calls it "not an irrational one." "Imagine if we could turn off the fear of making something lame. Imagine how much more we'd do." Hardy quoted. 20% of 100 vs 10% of 200 arithmetic. Ends by applying the thesis to itself ("our attitudes toward it are themselves early work").

**Modeling a Wealth Tax** (Aug 2020) — Median 8 words per sentence, mostly tables. .99^60 = .547. Ends: "A wealth tax compounds."

**The Four Quadrants of Conformism** (Jul 2020) — Opens with the classification and the axes. Kids as evidence: tattletales, sheep, dreamy ones, naughty ones. Birdcalls: "Crush <outgroup>!", "What will the neighbors think?", "To each his own.", "Eppur si muove." Minefield-on-the-pitch image. Ends: "imagination is, after all, their specialty."

**Orthodox Privilege** (Jul 2020) — Opens with an Einstein epigraph, then "There has been a lot of talk about privilege lately." Names the concept in sentence form and italic-style definition. "What an amazing coincidence that would be." Ends with the high-pitched-noise politeness analogy.

**Coronavirus and Credibility** (Apr 2020) — 236 words. Opens "I recently saw a video". Buffett quote. Ends with a two-word paragraph tail: "I hope."

**How to Write Usefully** (Feb 2020) — Opens with a 5-word question. Pike's Peak example of precision vs correctness. Robert Morris's trick. "My strategy is loose, then tight." Briars catching the sleeve. Defends "I think": "You need it to express your degree of certainty." "Brevity is the diction of command." "elegance and curtness are two names for the same thing." Ends: "If most essays are still unwritten, most such ideas are still undiscovered."

**Being a Noob** (Jan 2020) — Opens "When I was young, I thought old people had everything figured out. Now that I'm old, I know this isn't true." Farawavia. Hunger analogy. Ends: "the more you feel like a noob, the better."

**Haters** (Jan 2020) — 312 words, median 9.5. Two paragraphs identical with signs switched, and he tells you so. Ends: "Haters are just fanboys with the sign switched."

**The Two Kinds of Moderate** (Dec 2019) — Opens with the distinction in the first sentence. 0-to-100 scale. Ends: "be an accidental moderate, or be mediocre."

**Fashionable Problems** (Dec 2019) — 187 words. "essays, Lisp, venture funding — you may notice a pattern here."

**Having Kids** (Dec 2019) — Opens "Before I had kids, I was afraid of having kids." Median 11. "Better you than me." Crosswalk scene. Admits selection bias and quotes his own earlier sentence. "It hurts to see that sentence written down. I squirm to avoid it. But if there weren't something real there, why would I squirm?" "See what I did there?" Ends on other parents' faces.

**The Lesson to Unlearn** (Dec 2019) — Opens with the claim, 16 words. Blood-test analogy for exams. Reader-voice objection: "No, no, no, experienced students are saying to themselves." Founders wanting to launch on a Tuesday. "And then one day I realized this was not a rhetorical question." Ends with a society-scale prediction.

Batch 3 pattern: short essays end on the sentence that restates the title's idea in new words (Haters, Noob, Wealth Tax). He names his own devices while using them ("See what I did there?", "I'll save you the trouble"). Hedges are explicit and defended ("I think" expresses degree of certainty; "perhaps" held at arm's length), never vague.

## Batch 5 (Dec 2014 – Jul 2013)

**Let the Other 95% of Great Programmers In** (Dec 2014) — Opens with two sides of a debate and "Who's right?" Answer in the first words of para 2. "95 to 5." One quoted CEO: "We'd hire 30 tomorrow morning." Swears once, then acknowledges it: "I realize that's strong language." Ends with a five-word paragraph: "So please, get on with it."

**How to Be an Expert in a Changing World** (Dec 2014) — Opens with a conditional ("If the world were static"). One-sentence paragraph: "When experts are wrong, it's often because they're experts on an earlier version of the world." Ends "I don't expect that to change. But I could be wrong."

**How You Know** (Dec 2014) — Opens with Villehardouin's chronicle read "at least two times, maybe three." Hilbert quoted. "Your mind is like a compiled program you've lost the source of." Ends by extending to future tech.

**The Fatal Pinch** (Dec 2014) — Opens with a 39-word description of the situation. "That last sentence is the fatal one." "I try to resist coining phrases, but". **Mean People Fail** (Nov 2014) — Opens "It struck me recently how few of the most successful people I know are mean." Admits selection bias in para 4. Jessica as airport baggage scanner. **Before the Startup** (Oct 2014) — Talk. Opens with "what would I tell my own kids?" Skiing analogy for counterintuitive. Six numbered points with one-word headers. "playing house." ## Whole-corpus reading summary (all 21 batches, 232 essays, newest to oldest)

1. **Openers.** The commonest first sentence is a flat claim of 6 to 12 words or a dated personal fact. "I think it's possible to stop spam." "In the summer of 1995, my friend Robert Morris and I started a startup called Viaweb." Questions open 18 of 232. Epigraphs open a handful of the early ones. No opener sets a scene with "In today's world" or announces the essay's plan.
2. **First paragraph.** Median 45 words, 3 sentences. It states the claim or the anecdote and stops. The "why this matters" paragraph does not exist; the claim carries its own stakes.
3. **Argument shape.** State the received view, then complicate it ("Everyone knows X. What's less often understood is Y" in Beating the Averages). Give a wrong first version and fix it in public. Numbered lists when there are more than four points. Definitions stated as tests the reader can run (read the job listings; count the users; would you use it yourself).
4. **Examples.** Named people, dated events, unrounded numbers (79.7%, 1070 users, page 160, $5 per user). One physical analogy per abstraction, one clause or one sentence long, then dropped. Quotes others at length and by name.
5. **"I".** 5.5% of sentences start with "I". He uses "I think," "I suspect," "probably," "seems" heavily and deliberately, to mark degree of confidence, not to soften. He says "I don't know" and "I may be wrong" outright.
6. **Paragraph endings.** Short snap sentences: "Smack!" "Poof goes the axiom." "We did." "They just smelled wrong." Many paragraphs end on the concrete instance, not the generalization.
7. **Essay endings.** Restate the thesis in new words, return to the opening image or person, hand the reader a test, or end on two words ("It's time."). Talks recap; essays do not.
8. **Tics.** Sentence-initial "But" and "And" and "So." Colons before the payoff. Parenthetical asides. Em-dash interruptions. Contractions everywhere. "X is not Y. It's Z." Self-flagging of his own device ("I realize that's strong language").
