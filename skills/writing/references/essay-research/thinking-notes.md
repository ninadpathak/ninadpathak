# Thinking notes: how Paul Graham hooks and reasons, essay by essay

Source: https://paulgraham.com/articles.html, 232 essays refetched 2026-09-05 into /tmp/pg-essays/txt/ (stripped by /tmp/pg-essays/strip.py). Read newest to oldest for argument and hook, not sentences. Coverage: 106 essays, August 2026 (How Universities Should Prepare Founders) through August 2010 (What Happened to Yahoo). Reading stopped there at Ninad's direction on 2026-09-05; the remaining 126 older essays are in the corpus but not in these notes or in thinking.tsv. Sentence-level notes are in notes.md.

Machine-readable fields per essay live in thinking.tsv (slug, hook type, what the opening stands on, words before the thesis sentence, whether the ending returns to the hook, the thesis sentence). thinking-stats.py reads that file and the corpus and writes thinking-stats.json.

Hook type codes used in thinking.tsv and below:

- ANECDOTE: a first-person lived experience, usually dated ("A few weeks ago I...", "When I was a kid...").
- EVENT: a specific named person's remark, email, or talk, or a specific dated event involving others.
- BELIEF: a common or received view stated so it can be overturned or qualified.
- PUZZLE: a contradiction, anomaly, or coincidence he noticed and wants to explain.
- QUESTION: a question he was asked, or the title posed as a question, answered by the essay.
- DEFINITION: a definition or flat general claim, with no story in front of it.
- NUMBER: a count or measurement that surprised him.
- THOUGHT: a thought experiment or hypothetical ("Suppose you wanted to...", "Imagine...").
- AUDIENCE: a talk that opens by naming the room.

"Opens on" codes: SELF (his own experience), OTHERS (observation of specific other people or events), RECEIVED (a belief held by others), DEFINITION (a general claim or definition), HYPOTHETICAL.

Format per essay: **Title** (date) — hook type; verbatim hook; words to thesis; thesis; bridge; moves in order with one quoted line each; change of mind or admitted limit; how the ending relates to the opening.

## Batch 0 (Aug 2026 – Jan 2025)

**How Universities Should Prepare Founders** (Aug 2026) — QUESTION. Hook: "How should universities prepare students to start startups? Y Combinator is in the perfect position to answer this question, because we get them next. We're like grad school." Thesis at P4 (about 130 words in): "the way to prepare undergraduates to become successful founders is not to give them some new curriculum focused on 'entrepreneurship'. It's to do what universities already do best." Bridge: he inverts the question into one he can answer from data: "What do the YC partners look for? It's surprisingly simple." The specific case (what YC selects for) becomes the general claim (what universities should teach) because YC sits downstream. Moves: (1) invert the question, "What do the YC partners look for?"; (2) reason from a mechanism, "The hard part of startups is product... And that kind of knowledge comes from studying computer science"; (3) explain an anomaly with data, "Harvard alumni... apply at about twice the rate of Yale and Princeton alumni. Presumably Harvard students aren't that different... the reason... is just that it's more customary there"; (4) ask why, "But why is it so important for students to work on their own projects?"; (5) thought experiment, "Imagine what the US GDP would be if reading period at Harvard were twice as long"; (6) follow the implication further, "Which means a properly run class on how to start a startup would have to be a lab class"; (7) invert the received practice, "would-be founders should be doing exactly the opposite of what students do in business plan competitions." Limit admitted: "I would not want to draw a sharp line around them, because I can imagine other forms of building that could be useful." Ending: answers the opening question with a prediction, and names the cost of the answer (it looks like doing nothing). Returns to opening question: yes.

**How to Earn a Billion Dollars** (Jun 2026) — AUDIENCE, then EVENT. Hook: names the room ("Since this is apparently the future prime ministers' club"), then the trigger at P5: "So you can imagine how astonished I was last month when an American politician said that it was impossible to earn a billion dollars. I felt like a skating coach hearing someone say that it's impossible to do a triple axel." Thesis in the same paragraph: "Of course it's possible. It's hard, but it's possible." Bridge: P3-P4 establish standing (6500 companies, about 30 billionaires) before the remark, so the remark lands on someone with the data. Moves: (1) take the received view seriously by restating it precisely, "She wasn't saying, of course, that it's impossible to become a billionaire... What she meant was that it's impossible to get that rich without doing something bad"; (2) a case with a number, "93% last month, she said"; (3) supply the objection, "someone replied that having a few million and growing at 93% a month was radically different from being a billionaire"; (4) turn the objection into the lesson, "it turns out not merely to be false, but false in a very illuminating way"; (5) mechanism, "for you to grow 4000x, there has to be at least 4000x more demand"; (6) flag the counterintuitive step, "The answer is one of the most counterintuitive things about startups." Limit: none; the summary at the end is framed as a concession to the politicians. Ending: returns to the audience of the opening ("The ones I worry about are the future prime ministers") and restates the remark in reverse: "You not only don't have to cheat." Returns: yes.

**How to Convert Between Wealth and Income Tax** (May 2026) — QUESTION. Hook: the title as a question, 21 words, then "It's clear from the way most politicians talk about the subject that they not only don't know the answer, but don't even realize there's such a question." Thesis P3, 47 words in: "In fact the conversion rate between them is about 20." Bridge: the ignorance of others is the reason the question needs asking. Moves: (1) state the mechanism, "you have to divide by the rate of return on capital"; (2) worked example, "Suppose you have $100"; (3) follow the implication, "None of them would speak of adding a 'mere 20%' to the income tax rate, even though that's mathematically the same thing"; (4) test against real numbers, "Denmark, at 60.5%... Oklahoma's, which is 4.75%." Limit: "Historically that's an optimistic assumption. 4% might be more realistic. But 5% will do." Ending returns to the opening sentence about politicians not knowing the question exists: "The answer's not hard to understand, once you realize the question exists." Returns: yes.

**The Brand Age** (Mar 2026) — EVENT. Hook: "In the early 1970s, disaster struck the Swiss watch industry. Now people call it the quartz crisis, but in fact it was a compound of three separate disasters." Thesis at end of P6 (about 330 words in): "the way they did it was by transforming themselves from precision instrument makers into luxury brands." Bridge: the correction in sentence two ("but in fact it was a compound of three") is the bridge; the essay earns the general claim by getting the history more exactly right than the received label. Moves: (1) correct the received name; (2) distinguish two goals, "There were two things watchmakers sought above all in the golden age: thinness and accuracy"; (3) ask why with a date, "Why did they suddenly decide to make their brand shout, after a century of whispering?"; (4) enumerate the only options, "There are only two ways to combine branding and good design"; (5) ask why-here, "Why did it happen with mechanical watches? Because the wristwatch turns out to be the perfect vehicle for it"; (6) time-machine thought experiment, "imagine what someone from the golden age would notice if we brought him here in a time machine"; (7) explain the anomaly, "the reason it's strange is that there's no function for form to follow"; (8) reject his own first explanation, "That's not why brand age watches look strange." Limit: "I haven't talked about Rolex so far." Ending: from the disaster of the opening to "golden age" as a general thing, closing with a rule ("Follow the problems") and "call it a golden age." Returns: yes, to the golden age the disaster ended.

**The Shape of the Essay Field** (Jun 2025) — DEFINITION. Hook: "An essay has to tell people something they don't already know. But there are three different reasons people might not know something." Thesis P5, about 230 words in: "If you're writing for smart people about important things, you're writing for the young." Bridge, stated outright: "The reason I did this breakdown was to get at the following fact, which might have seemed controversial if I'd led with it, but should be obvious now." Moves: (1) taxonomy, "(a) that it's not important, (b) that they're obtuse, or (c) that they're inexperienced"; (2) ask why, "you have to ask why your readers don't already know them"; (3) sharpen, "Or more precisely, that's where you'll have the most effect"; (4) mechanism as a product, "The impact of an essay is how much it changes readers' thinking multiplied by the importance of the topic"; (5) apply to himself, "I noticed empirically that I seemed to be writing for the young." Change of mind: "In fact I only really figured it out as I was writing this essay." Ending opens a new question ("What would surprise young readers?... I should think about that."). Returns to hook: no.

**Good Writing** (May 2025) — DEFINITION, with the thesis in the first paragraph. Hook: "There are two senses in which writing can be good: it can sound good, and the ideas can be right... It might seem as if these two kinds of good would be unrelated, like the speed of a car and the color it's painted. And yet I don't think they are." Thesis, 71 words in: "I think writing that sounds good is more likely to be right." Bridge: "So here we have the most exciting kind of idea: one that seems both preposterous and true. Let's examine it. How can this possibly be true?" Moves: (1) evidence from his own practice, "I know it's true from writing"; (2) tighten the definition, "By right I mean more than just true"; (3) anecdote as clue, "something I noticed 30 years ago when I was doing the layout for my first book"; (4) analogy as mechanism, "when you shake a bin full of different objects... Gravity won't let them become less tightly packed, so any change has to be a change for the better"; (5) turn the analogy into a heuristic, "you can use getting the rhythm right as a heuristic for getting the ideas right"; (6) bound the claim, "It doesn't apply when you have ideas in some other way and then write about them afterward"; (7) objection in the reader's voice ("but what about liars?"). Change of mind: "So it's not quite right to say that better sounding writing is more likely to be true. Better sounding writing is more likely to be internally consistent." Ending returns to the car of P1 ("more like the shape of a plane than the color of a car") and closes on the thesis inverted: "It's hard to be right without sounding right." Returns: yes.

**What to Do** (Mar 2025) — QUESTION. Hook: "What should one do? That may seem a strange question, but it's not meaningless or unanswerable. It's the sort of question kids ask before they learn not to ask big questions." Thesis P2, about 60 words in: "Make good new things." Bridge: "So what should one do? One should help people, and take care of the world. Those two are obvious. But is there anything else?" The two obvious answers clear the ground for the one he wants. Moves: (1) admit unprovability then give a reason anyway, "I can't prove that one should do this... But I can explain why this principle makes sense"; (2) revise his own wording in public, "the first version of this principle was to have good new ideas. But that didn't seem general enough"; (3) test against cases, "it didn't include making art or music"; (4) ask whether a part is essential, "Is newness essential? I think so"; (5) follow the implication, "Which in turn implies it's not impressive to make the same thing over and over." Limit: "We're talking about first principles here." Ending is the last implication; no return to the kid asking the question. Returns: no.

**The Origins of Wokeness** (Jan 2025) — DEFINITION. Hook: "The word 'prig' isn't very common now, but if you look up the definition, it will sound familiar." Thesis P3, about 40 words in: "although wokeness is a comparatively recent phenomenon, it's an instance of a much older one." Bridge: "its age is an important clue." Moves: (1) reframe the question, "the question to ask is not why people behave this way. Every society has prigs. The question to ask is why our prigs are priggish about these ideas, at this moment"; (2) define, "An aggressively performative focus on social justice. In other words, it's people being prigs about social justice"; (3) answer where by elimination, "Did it begin in math, or the hard sciences...? Those are amusing images, but no"; (4) demand a theory explain the non-event, "A successful theory of the origin of political correctness has to be able to explain why it didn't happen earlier"; (5) mechanism, "for it to work as a substitute for virtue, orthodoxy must be difficult"; (6) thought experiments, "Imagine having to explain to a well-meaning visitor from another planet" and "imagine we were dealing with some other religion, like Christianity." Limits: "I don't know if it actually was, but people thought it was"; "I'm not going to claim Trump's second victory in 2024 was a referendum on wokeness." Ending is a rule about heresies; the prig returns only as "the aggressively conventional-minded." Returns: no.

## Batch 1 (Oct 2024 – Oct 2023)

**Writes and Write-Nots** (Oct 2024) — DEFINITION, a prediction, with the thesis inside the first sentence: "I'm usually reluctant to make predictions about technology, but I feel fairly confident about this one: in a couple decades there won't be many people who can write." The hedge is the hook; a reluctant predictor predicting is news. Bridge: "One of the strangest things you learn if you're a writer is how many people have trouble writing." Moves: (1) ask why, "The reason so many people have trouble writing is that it's fundamentally difficult"; (2) two opposing forces as mechanism, "the pervasive expectation of writing and the irreducible difficulty of doing it, create enormous pressure"; (3) explain an anomaly with the mechanism, "This is why eminent professors often turn out to have resorted to plagiarism... Which means they're not even halfway decent at writing"; (4) supply the objection, "Is that so bad? Isn't it common for skills to disappear when technology makes them obsolete? There aren't many blacksmiths left"; (5) answer with a reason already planted, "Yes, it's bad. The reason is something I mentioned earlier: writing is thinking"; (6) follow the implication one more step, "It will be a world of thinks and think-nots." Limit: none stated. Ending carries the analogy to its last step ("only those who choose to be") and restates the prediction. Returns: yes.

**When To Do What You Love** (Sep 2024) — QUESTION, a debated one. Hook: "There's some debate about whether it's a good idea to 'follow your passion.' In fact the question is impossible to answer with a simple yes or no." Thesis, 48 words in: "The only way to give a general answer is to trace it." Bridge: he finds the hidden assumption in the question: "When people talk about this question, there's always an implicit 'instead of.'" Moves: (1) notice the implicit assumption; (2) name the exception, "But there's an obvious exception: when you both want the same thing"; (3) find the edge case that inverts the advice, "there's an edge case here so spectacular that it turns all the preceding advice on its head"; (4) ask why, "Why is this pattern so common? Because the best ideas tend to be such outliers that you'd overlook them if you were consciously looking for ways to make money"; (5) what if, "What if you're not sure what you want?"; (6) explain the received advice as selection bias, "There's a lot of selection bias in advice about whether to 'follow your passion,' and this is the reason"; (7) necessary versus sufficient, "This is not a sufficient condition for doing great work, but it is a necessary one." Ending: the one case where the traced border is simple, "The answer is yes." Returns to the opening question: yes.

**Founder Mode** (Sep 2024) — EVENT. Hook: "At a YC event last week Brian Chesky gave a talk that everyone who was there will remember... Ron Conway, for the first time in his life, forgot to take notes. I'm not going to try to reproduce it here. Instead I want to talk about a question it raised." Thesis P5, about 330 words in: "In effect there are two different ways to run a company: founder mode and manager mode." Bridge: the one talk becomes a pattern, "one after another said that the same thing had happened to them." Moves: (1) ask why about the pattern, "Why was everyone telling these founders the wrong thing? That was the big mystery to me"; (2) explain the anomaly, "what they were being told was how to run a company you hadn't founded"; (3) infer an unseen thing from its effects, "we can infer the existence of another mode from the dismay of founders who've tried it"; (4) admit ignorance as content, "There are as far as I know no books specifically about founder mode. Business schools don't know it exists"; (5) thought experiment, "Can you imagine the force of will it would take to do this at the average company?" Limit: "We still don't know. That's how little we know about founder mode." Ending: "like Steve Jobs instead of John Sculley," returning to the Jobs study named in P2. Returns: yes.

**The Right Kind of Stubborn** (Jul 2024) — PUZZLE: two things that look alike. Hook: "Successful people tend to be persistent... Mere obstinacy, on the other hand, is a recipe for failure... But is there any real difference between these two cases?" Thesis P5, about 150 words in: "I've talked to a lot of determined people, and it seems to me that they're different kinds of behavior." Bridge: he says what would have to be true for the question to matter: "If that's the only difference then there's nothing to be learned from the distinction." Moves: (1) distinguish two lookalikes; (2) test with a named case, "I can't think of anyone more determined than the Collison brothers, and when you point out a problem to them, they not only listen, but listen with an almost predatory intensity"; (3) mechanism, "The reason the persistent and the obstinate seem similar is that they're both hard to stop. But they're hard to stop in different senses"; (4) degenerate case, "In the degenerate case they're indistinguishable: when there's only one way to solve a problem"; (5) explain why the culture conflates them, "This is presumably why the two are so often conflated in popular culture. It assumes simple problems"; (6) decompose, "Five distinct qualities — energy, imagination, resilience, good judgement, and focus on a goal"; (7) implication, "Which means the persistent must also be imaginative." Limit: "It must be a feature of one's personality" is offered as a guess. Ending returns to the opening pair: "Anyone can do obstinacy. Indeed, kids and drunks and fools are best at it." Returns: yes.

**The Reddits** (Mar 2024) — ANECDOTE. Hook: "I met the Reddits before we even started Y Combinator. In fact they were one of the reasons we started it." Thesis is that second sentence, 10 words in. Bridge: chronology; each paragraph is a scene. Moves: (1) correct his own earlier belief in passing, "since we thought then that we were funding ideas rather than founders, we rejected them"; (2) reason from a mechanism, "It turns out you don't need that many users to collect a decent list of interesting links"; (3) which-is-why, "Which is why, despite a long stretch after Steve left when the management strategy ranged from benign neglect to spectacular blunders, traffic just kept growing"; (4) pose the question the story answers, "what could it do if Steve came back?" Limit: "Or at least a lower bound on the answer." Ending returns to Steve of the opening: "Steve is not out of ideas yet." Returns: yes.

**How to Start Google** (Mar 2024) — BELIEF (a talk). Hook: "Most of you probably think that when you're released into the so-called real world you'll eventually have to get some kind of job. That's not true." Thesis P3, about 45 words in: "The trick is to start your own company." Bridge: "today I'm going to talk about a trick you can use to avoid ever having to get a job." Moves: (1) anticipate the reader's thought and turn it, "You might be thinking 'How could we start Google?' But that's effectively what the people who did start Google were thinking before they started it"; (2) define the claim exactly, "how to get to the point where you can start a company that has as much chance of being Google as Google had of being Google"; (3) that's-why, "That's why tech companies all make you take a coding test before they'll hire you"; (4) thought experiments, "Imagine you're writing a game" and "Let's suppose you're 22 and you've succeeded"; (5) analogy as reasoning, "any carpenter looking at this situation would think 'why don't you just plane off the part that sticks?'"; (6) that's-why again, "That's why the best startups grow out of projects that aren't meant to be startups." Ending: a two-item recap, flagged as such because it is a talk to kids. Returns to the job hook: no.

**The Best Essay** (Mar 2024) — QUESTION, posed against its own title. Hook: "Despite its title this isn't meant to be the best essay. My goal here is to figure out what the best essay would be like." Thesis P4, about 85 words in: "The best essay would be on the most important topic you could tell people something surprising about." Bridge: "A good essay has to be surprising. It has to tell people something they don't already know." Moves: (1) follow the consequences, "That may sound obvious, but it has some unexpected consequences. One is that science enters the picture like an elephant stepping into a rowboat"; (2) change his mind in public, "I imagined when I started writing this that the best essay would be fairly timeless... But in fact the opposite seems to be true"; (3) notice he asked the wrong question, "then I started with the wrong question"; (4) replace it with a near twin whose answer diverges, "Instead of asking what would the best essay be? I should have asked how do you write essays well?"; (5) mechanism, "Usually there are several possible responses to a question, which means you're traversing a tree. But essays are linear"; (6) distinguish two senses, "There are two senses in which an essay can be timeless"; (7) accept a strange answer, "to be the evergreen kind of timeless, an essay has to be ineffective." Limit: "In this essay I've already cut a 17-paragraph subtree." Ending: "How to get more questions? That is the most important question of all." Returns to hook: no; it returns to the replaced question.

**Superlinear Returns** (Oct 2023) — ANECDOTE, with the thesis inside it. Hook: "One of the most important things I didn't understand about the world when I was a child is the degree to which the returns for performance are superlinear." Bridge: the received view quoted and killed by mechanism, "'You get out,' I heard a thousand times, 'what you put in.'... If your product is only half as good as your competitor's, you don't get half as many customers. You get no customers, and you go out of business." Moves: (1) refute the received view with a mechanism; (2) take the objection seriously, "Some think this is a flaw of capitalism... But superlinear returns for performance are a feature of the world, not an artifact of rules we've invented"; (3) reduce many cases to two causes, "they reduce to two fundamental causes: exponential growth and thresholds"; (4) register his own surprise as evidence, "Even after decades of thinking about this, I find that sentence startling"; (5) this-is-why, "This is why history is full of empires"; (6) test advice against a what-if, "'Seek competition' is similarly useless; what if the prize isn't worth competing for?"; (7) implication, "Which means it's not just a bargain to do exceptional work, but a bargain even to try to." Limit: "as far as I can tell." Ending: curiosity over ambition, "it may grow into a mountain beneath you." Returns to the childhood hook: no.

## Batch 2 (Jul 2023 – Feb 2022)

**How to Do Great Work** (Jul 2023) — QUESTION, a method question he answers by doing the work. Hook: "If you collected lists of techniques for doing great work in a lot of different fields, what would the intersection look like? I decided to find out by making it." Thesis P2, about 60 words in: "it does have a definite shape; it's not just a point labelled 'work hard.'" Bridge: "I decided to find out by making it." Moves: (1) admit what can't be proved and give the evidence anyway, "It may not be possible to prove that you have to work hard to do great things, but the empirical evidence is on the scale of the evidence for mortality"; (2) explain a surprise by mechanism, "The reason we're surprised is that we underestimate the cumulative effect of work"; (3) ask why a widely observed thing is true, "This observation has been made by so many people in so many different fields that it might be worth thinking about why it's true"; (4) split into two kinds, "There are two ways to be comfortable breaking rules: to enjoy breaking them, and to be indifferent to them"; (5) invert the question, "ask what would be good ideas for someone else to explore"; (6) ask why nobody did it, "Why did no one think of this before?"; (7) follow the implication to a paradox, "Which means that originality consists partly of puzzlement — of confusion!" Limit: "The reason I mention this case explicitly is that so many people get it wrong." Ending: "The discoveries are out there, waiting to be made. Why not by you?" Returns to the intersection hook: no.

**How to Get New Ideas** (Jan 2023) — DEFINITION. **What You (Want to)\* Want** (Nov 2022) — PUZZLE, held since age 9. Hook: "Since I was about 9 I've been puzzled by the apparent contradiction between being made of matter that behaves in a predictable way, and the feeling that I could choose to do whatever I wanted." Thesis P2, about 140 words in, given as a wrong version: "You can do what you want, but you can't want what you want." Bridge, stated as method: "The best way to explain the answer may be to start with a slightly wrong version, and then fix it." Moves: (1) wrong version; (2) counterexample he supplies, "The reason this is mistaken is that people do sometimes change what they want. People who don't want to want something — drug addicts, for example — can sometimes make themselves stop wanting it"; (3) revise, "So we modify our initial statement"; (4) counterexample again, "That's still not quite true... 'I decided to stop wanting to like classical music'"; (5) generalize the iteration with an analogy, "in much the same way we can get arbitrarily close to 1 by adding more 9s"; (6) express as a regex. Change of mind is the essay's form. Ending answers the childhood puzzle: "Ultimately you get back to a want that you don't control." Returns: yes.

**The Need to Read** (Nov 2022) — ANECDOTE. Hook: "In the science fiction books I read as a kid, reading had often been replaced by some more efficient way of acquiring knowledge. Mysterious 'tapes' would load it into one's brain." Thesis P2, about 70 words in: "Reading about x doesn't just teach you about x; it also teaches you how to write." Bridge: "even if one existed, it would be insufficient." Moves: (1) ask whether it matters, "Would that matter? If we replaced reading, would anyone need to be good at writing?"; (2) reason, "The reason it would matter is that writing is not just a way to convey ideas, but also a way to have them"; (3) bound the claim, "There are of course kinds of thinking that can be done without writing... If you're thinking about how two pieces of machinery should fit together, writing about it probably won't help much"; (4) follow the implication, "Which in turn means that someone who's not good at writing will almost always be at a disadvantage." Ending returns to the tapes: "People who just want information may find other ways to get it. But people who want to have ideas can't afford to." Returns: yes.

**Alien Truth** (Oct 2022) — THOUGHT. Hook: "If there were intelligent beings elsewhere in the universe, they'd share certain truths in common with us." Thesis P1, about 50 words in: "I think we'd share other truths with aliens besides the truths of math and physics, and that it would be worthwhile to think about what these might be." Bridge: examples, "I think we'd share the principle that a controlled experiment testing some hypothesis entitles us to have proportionally increased belief in it." Moves: (1) admit the limit up front, "We can only guess, of course"; (2) sharpen the purpose, "a threshold, or more precisely a target, for truth"; (3) set a heuristic rule, "Alien truth will work best as a heuristic if we err on the side of generosity"; (4) relate to a known idea, "The idea of alien truth is related to Erdos's idea of God's book"; (5) name it and hold the name loosely, "The obvious choice is 'philosophy.'... But I'm not wedded to that"; (6) bet on his own claim in a parenthesis, "(Let's see if that one does.)" Ending applies the idea to itself: "that is itself probably an alien truth." Returns: yes.

**What I've Learned from Users** (Sep 2022) — ANECDOTE. Hook: "I recently told applicants to Y Combinator that the best advice I could give for getting in, per word, was / Explain what you've learned from users." Thesis P5, about 75 words in: "most startups have the same problems." Bridge: he turns his own test on himself, "Afterward I asked myself the same question. What have I learned from YC's users, the startups we've funded?" Moves: (1) surprise as evidence, "It was a surprise to me how often the same problems recur"; (2) explain an anomaly with a mechanism, "How could things be fine at 60 startups and broken at 80? It was only a third more. Then I realized what had happened. We were using an O(n2) algorithm"; (3) dig to the real reason under the stated one, "the difficulties they're having raising money, and after digging into their situation, it turns out the reason is that the company is doing badly"; (4) ask why repeatedly, "It took me a long time to figure out why founders don't listen... the reason founders don't listen to us is that they don't believe us... The reason startups are so counterintuitive is that they're so different"; (5) which-is-why, "Which is why YC partners should usually have been founders themselves"; (6) ask why again, "Why are founders uncertain about what to do?" Limit: "I didn't know it when we started YC." Ending on founders' generosity; no return to the applicants. Returns: no.

**Heresy** (Apr 2022) — ANECDOTE: the same passage read twice. Hook: "One of the most surprising things I've witnessed in my lifetime is the rebirth of the concept of heresy." Then Westfall on Newton, then: "The first time I read that, in the 1990s, it sounded amusingly medieval...
