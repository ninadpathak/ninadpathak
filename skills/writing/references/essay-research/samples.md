## Sample A: Why Interior Contractors Get Away With It

Thinking checklist:

1. Observed: Ninad paid about 20 lakh rupees to an interior contractor on his own home in [year]; [what was delivered, and the moment the money was understood to be gone].
2. Puzzle: some people cheat in every trade, yet in this trade the ones who cheat keep getting hired. A restaurant that poisons ten diners closes. A contractor who walks off ten jobs is booked.
3. Received belief and holders: homeowners and the press say contractors are greedy or that Indians don't enforce contracts. Ninad believed the first until [the moment he stopped].
4. Mechanism: the homeowner is a one-shot customer who pays in advance for work that gets hidden behind plaster. Reputation can't compound because no later customer can see the earlier job.
5. Counterexample to answer: contracts exist, and some homeowners sue. Answer: [Ninad had a contract]; a contract is memory for one customer, not for the market.
6. What would have to be true: if the mechanism is right, the same contractor should be fine in a trade with repeat customers, and the bad ones should cluster where each customer buys once. [Check against Interio's intake data.]
7. What it also predicts: the fix is a shared record, not a stricter contract. If stricter contracts alone had worked, the problem would already be smaller in the cities where courts are fastest. [Verify.]
8. Nervous implication: certification only works once losing it costs a contractor a job, so the mark has to be withheld, not just awarded.
9. What would change my mind: a city where a public complaints record exists and the fraud rate is unchanged.
10. Ending returns to: the 20 lakh, as the price of a memory the market didn't have.

In [year] I paid an interior contractor about 20 lakh rupees to renovate my home. [What he delivered, one sentence.] [The day I understood the rest wasn't coming, one sentence.] When I told people, they didn't look surprised. [N] of them had the same story with a different sum.

The puzzle isn't that some contractors cheat. Some people in every trade do. The puzzle is that the ones who cheat keep getting hired. A restaurant that poisons ten diners is empty the next month. A contractor who walks off ten jobs has a full calendar. What's different?

Two things, and neither is greed. First, you renovate a home about once in [ten] years. So the contractor never sees you again, and he knows it on the day he quotes. Reputation compounds only when customers come back or talk to each other, and homeowners do neither. Second, you pay in advance for work you can't inspect. The wiring goes behind the plaster before you can check it. By the time the tiles crack, the advance is spent on a client in [another city] who has never heard your name.

Which means the fix isn't a stricter contract. [I had a contract.] A contract is memory for one customer. The trade needs memory for the market: a record that follows a contractor from one home to the next, so the tenth job sees the first nine. That's what a trust mark is, and it's what InterioHQ certified is meant to become. [How the vetting works, one sentence.] The first time a contractor loses a job because he isn't certified, the trade has a memory. Mine cost 20 lakh.

## Sample B: 9 Agentic Search Tools Ranked by Index Points per Dollar

Thinking checklist:

1. Observed: in [month] the [tool] invoice was [$X] against [N] answered questions, so [$Y] per answer.
2. Puzzle: vendors publish price per thousand queries and index size; reviewers publish latency. No one divides one by the other, yet the division is the only number a buyer needs.
3. Received belief and holders: buyers and reviewers rank by price per query and latency. [Ninad did too, until the invoice.]
4. Mechanism: a query is a lookup, and a lookup is worth what the table behind it holds. A cheap miss is a purchase of nothing.
5. Counterexample to answer: a small index tuned to your domain can beat a large general one. Answer: then measure index reached for your queries, which is still index per dollar with a narrower numerator.
6. What would have to be true: if the ratio is right, the cheapest tool by price per query should not be the cheapest by index per dollar. [Check the nine.]
7. What it also predicts: two tools with the same price per query and different indexes should give different hit rates on the same questions. [Run it.]
8. Nervous implication: the most expensive tool by list price may be the cheapest by this ratio, which makes the ranking look like an advertisement. Publish the arithmetic.
9. What would change my mind: a tool whose small index wins on hit rate for the questions the agent actually asks.
10. Ending returns to: the invoice.

In [month] our search bill for [tool] came to [$X]. The agent it fed had answered [N] questions. I did the division. Each answer had cost [$Y], about the price of [a physical thing that costs $Y]. That seemed wrong, so I ran the division on the other eight tools.

What you're buying from a search tool is the index a query can reach, so the only price that matters is index per dollar. The vendors publish price per thousand queries, and they publish index size. Reviewers publish latency. None of them divides one by the other. But a query that misses isn't cheaper because it came back fast. Index per dollar ranks the nine tools below, and it's not the order of the price lists.

Why would the ranking differ from the price lists? Not because the cheap tools are slow. [Cheapest tool] returned in [N ms], faster than [most expensive tool]. The reason is that a query is a lookup, and a lookup is worth what the table behind it holds. [Tool A] charges [$A] per thousand queries against an index of [P] pages. [Tool B] charges [$B] against [Q] pages: [k] times the index for [j] times the price. Price per query hides that gap. Index per dollar doesn't.
