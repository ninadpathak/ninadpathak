## 1. The article, and why this one

**Picked: `how-to-write-a-technical-tutorial-that-actually-teaches`.**

It is the only article this campaign has published that already holds a top-10 position on a
real human query. Search Console, 90 days to 2026-08-14, human non-brand:

| Query | Impressions | Position |
|---|---:|---:|
| how to create tech tutorials | 3 | **7.0** |
| technical tutorial | 5 | **10.8** |
| technical tutorials | 2 | 28.0 |
| how to write a tutorial | 2 | 27.5 |
| how to write tutorial | 1 | 42.0 |

Five named queries is the widest query surface of any campaign article, and two of them are at
or beside page one.

**That last fact is the entire reason to test video here.** The campaign's diagnosed bottleneck
is position: only 5.9% of human impressions have ever been in the top 10, and 33 of 90 posts
have ever earned a human impression while **zero have ever earned a human click**. This page is
the single exception to the first half of that — it is already at position 7 — and it still has
zero clicks. A page at position 7 that earns nothing is not a ranking problem. It is a
click problem, and a click problem at a won position is the one thing on this site that a
richer result could plausibly fix.

### Why not the alternatives

- **`technical-documentation-template`** — highest impressions of any article at 30, but its
  queries sit at position 74.5 and 79.3. Video does not move a page from 74 to 10, and picking
  it would test nothing: no change is measurable from there.
- **`how-to-organize-a-documentation-site`** — the most visually native subject on the site, and
  the best page-level position at 14.0. Rejected because **no query-level row survives the
  join**, so there is no named query to measure against. It is the right second test once
  something is known about the first.
- **`writing-release-notes-that-developers-trust`** — 11 impressions, but position 62.5 on its
  one named query. Same problem as the template page.

### Why the subject suits motion

The article is a process with a failure at its centre: a reader follows a tutorial exactly and
step three breaks, because the setup the writer's own machine supplied was never written down.
That is a sequence with a fault in it, which is the one thing prose is genuinely worse at than
motion — prose has to describe the break in the order the sentences run, and motion can show
the break and its cause in the same instant.

It is also self-demonstrating. A video that teaches this well is evidence for the argument it
is making. If it fails to teach, that is a finding too.

## 2. The spine

What the viewer understands, and when. If a viewer leaves at any of these marks they should
have something whole, not a fragment.

| Mark | The viewer now understands |
|---|---|
| **0:15** | Tutorials fail at the setup the writer never wrote down, because their own machine already had it. |
| **1:00** | A tutorial is one of four modes, and it needs two fixed endpoints — a written starting state and a visible finish line — before any prose exists. |
| **2:00** | The path is walked first and outlined second; prerequisites are checks, examples are complete, one real failure is included, and everything off the main path is cut. |
| **End** | Testing from a clean environment is what proves it, because that is when the writer's invisible setup falls out and becomes visible as gaps. |

Someone who watches instead of reading has the whole argument. There is no "read the article
for the rest" beat, and no call to action that withholds anything.

## 3. The film: what transforms, beat by beat

**The governing rule: the tutorial document is the character.** It is on screen almost the
whole time, and it is built, broken, and repaired. Nothing here is a card with text on it. If a
beat below could be screenshotted and lose nothing, it is the wrong beat and should be sent
back.

### Beat 1 — 0:00–0:18 · The break

A tutorial renders as a vertical trail of numbered steps, drawn top to bottom as if being read.
A small reader-mark travels down it. Step one passes. Step two passes. **On step three the trail
stops and the step's top border goes accent-orange.**

Then the step splits horizontally and four items rise out from underneath it, from the space
where they were always hiding: a permission, a package version, a running service, a file. They
were never on the page. They were on the writer's machine.

*Why motion beats prose:* the cause is spatially underneath the symptom, and both are visible
in the same frame. Prose has to put them in separate sentences and the reader has to hold the
first one while reading the second.

### Beat 2 — 0:18–0:42 · Four modes, three discarded

The broken document contracts to a single shape, then **divides into four** shapes that sort
themselves apart. Each takes a distinct silhouette: the tutorial keeps the trail form, how-to
collapses to a single step, reference becomes a dense grid, explanation becomes flowing prose.
Three of them slide out of frame. The tutorial trail remains and re-expands.

*Why motion:* the four modes are usually a table, and a table invites the viewer to read four
cells and compare them. Making them physically different shapes that separate from one object
says the thing the table only implies — these are the same content forced into different forms,
and picking wrong is a shape error, not a wording error.

**Attribution requirement:** the four-mode split is the Diátaxis framework and must be named on
screen and in narration. It is external work the article cites and links, not ours.

### Beat 3 — 0:42–1:05 · Two endpoints

Two markers set down at opposite ends of the frame, and the trail between them is *absent* —
just empty space. The left marker unfolds into a written starting state, item by item. The
right marker resolves into the finished result, shown early and then held, dimmed, at the edge
of frame for the rest of the film as the thing being aimed at.

*Why motion:* the finish line staying visible in the periphery for ninety seconds does the work
of the article's "show the completed result early" advice, continuously, without restating it.

### Beat 4 — 1:05–1:31 · The path is walked before it is labelled

A line draws itself from the left marker toward the right one — **hesitantly, with backtracks**,
one segment retracing itself before continuing. This is someone doing the task. Only once the
line reaches the finish do the headings arrive: they fly in and **snap onto segments that
already exist**, as labels.

Then the same sequence runs again in reverse and fails: headings arrive first, arranged neatly,
and the line that tries to connect them cannot — it dead-ends between two headings and the gap
sits there.

*Why motion:* this is the article's least intuitive claim and its most important one. The order
of two operations is the whole argument, and order is what motion encodes natively. In prose it
is a sentence that readers nod at and then ignore.

### Beat 5 — 1:31–1:53 · Checks, complete examples, one real failure

Prerequisites drop in ahead of the trail's start as a row of checks that flip to a passed state
one by one; the trail will not begin until the last one flips. A code block on the path opens
and **fills in its own missing lines** from the edges inward, becoming complete in place. Then
one step deliberately turns to the error state, holds long enough to be read, and resolves —
the trail continues through it rather than around it.

*Why motion:* "complete at the point of use" is shown by a fragment becoming whole where it
stands, which is a transformation. A card reading "use complete examples" is the slideshow this
brief exists to prevent.

### Beat 6 — 1:53–2:08 · Pruning

Branches sprout off the main trail — alternatives, options, asides — and the trail visibly sags
and lengthens under them. Then the branches are **pruned**, one at a time, and with each cut the
main path straightens and shortens until it is a single clean line.

*Why motion:* cost and relief. The sag communicates what a side quest does to a reader's
progress, and no sentence in the article makes that felt.

### Beat 7 — 2:08–2:30 · Clean environment, and the seal

The whole document is lifted and set down inside an empty container. Everything the writer's
machine had been silently supplying **falls out** and lands as visible gaps punched through the
trail — including, in the same position it occupied in beat 1, the gap at step three.

The gaps fill. The reader-mark runs the trail again, end to end, without stopping. The step-three
break from the cold open seals shut.

*Why motion:* the film closes the loop it opened, in the same spatial position. That is
structure the article cannot have, because prose has no memory of where something was on screen.

## 4. Narration script

Draft. **This is prose, so it goes to the Codex slop gate before a frame is animated.** The
falsifiability standard applies exactly as it does on the page: could a reader who knows the
subject point at a sentence and say "that is bullshit". Every line below is either a claim the
article already makes or a statement about the general shape of the problem. There are no
numbers, no named companies, no claim about anything Ninad personally measured.

```
0:00  Someone is following your tutorial exactly as written, and step three fails.
0:06  The missing piece is usually a permission, a package version, a running service,
      or a file the page never mentioned.
0:13  None of it was in the tutorial. All of it was on the writer's machine.

0:19  So before anything else, decide this should be a tutorial at all.
0:24  The Diátaxis framework separates documentation into four modes. A tutorial teaches
      by building something all the way through. A how-to completes one task. Reference
      states exact facts. Explanation gives background.
0:36  If the reader only needs an answer, a tutorial is the wrong shape, and no amount
      of editing fixes the shape.

0:43  A tutorial needs two fixed points: where the reader starts, and what they will
      have built when they finish.
0:51  Write the starting state down explicitly. It is the part writers skip, because
      their own machine already satisfies it.
0:59  Show the finished result early, so the reader knows what they are aiming at.

1:06  Now build the path. Build it before you write the outline.
1:12  Do the task yourself, start to finish, and record what you actually did, including
      the parts you got wrong.
1:20  The headings come afterwards, as labels for steps that already exist.
1:26  Outline first and you produce a path nobody has walked.

1:32  Prerequisites become checks rather than prose. Each one either passes or it does not.
1:39  Code examples are complete where they are used, because a fragment sends the reader
      hunting for the rest.
1:46  Include one meaningful failure and its fix. A reader who has already seen the error
      recognises it when it is their turn.

1:54  Then cut. Every choice offered in the main path is a decision the reader has to make
      while they are still learning.
2:02  Side quests come out. Alternatives go somewhere else.

2:09  Finally, run it from a clean environment.
2:14  Everything your own machine was quietly supplying falls out, and those gaps are
      exactly what the reader was going to hit.
2:21  Fill them, then watch someone else run it and narrate their confusion out loud.
2:28  Step three stops failing.
```

**Read-rate note for the builder:** this is ~330 words over 150 seconds, about 130 words per
minute. That is deliberately below conversational pace, because the frame is doing half the
work and the viewer needs room to watch it. Do not compress the film to fit faster narration.

## 5. Design tokens — actual values, not "match the site"

Extracted from `static/css/main.css`. **Use the dark palette.** Video is watched on a
dark-dominant surface, the site's dark theme is the higher-contrast of the two, and the accent
reads far better against `#090909` than against `#f8f8f6`.

```
BACKGROUND        --bg        #090909      frame ground
SURFACE           --bg-1      #111111      step cards, code blocks
SURFACE RAISED    --bg-2      #1a1a1a      headers, prerequisite chips
BORDER            --border    #1e1e1e      hairlines at rest
BORDER STRONG     --border-2  #2c2c2c      trail lines, connectors
TEXT PRIMARY      --text-1    #f0f0f0      step titles, narration captions
TEXT SECONDARY    --text-2    #c2c2c2      step detail
TEXT TERTIARY     --text-3    #6a6a6a      kickers, labels, timestamps
ACCENT            --accent    #ff5f1f      active step, progress, the break
ACCENT WASH       --accent-lo #1a0d07      fill behind an active step
ERROR             #ff8a80                  the deliberate failure in beat 5
SUCCESS           #6ee7a7                  a prerequisite check that passes
```

```
SANS   Inter — weights 400, 500, 600 only
MONO   JetBrains Mono — step numbers, code, edge labels, prerequisites

TYPE SCALE (rem at 16px base; scale up proportionally for 1080p)
  step title        1rem      / 1.45   weight 600
  step detail       0.8125rem / 1.55   weight 400
  edge label        0.6875rem / 1.4    weight 600, mono, letter-spacing 0.08em
  section title     1.5rem    / 1.3    weight 600
  narration caption 1.125rem  / 1.4    weight 400

SPACING RHYTHM — use only these, they are the site's scale
  4, 8, 12, 16, 20, 24, 32, 40, 48, 64, 80, 96 px

RADIUS   4px everywhere. 6px for the one container that needs to read as a panel. Nothing else.
EASING   cubic-bezier(0.16, 1, 0.3, 1)   ← this is the site's --ease, use it for every move
BASE DURATION  160ms for state changes (--t). Structural moves: 400–700ms. Never linear.
```

**The easing curve is not decoration.** `cubic-bezier(0.16, 1, 0.3, 1)` is heavily
front-loaded — things arrive fast and settle slowly. Every transform in this film should use it
so the motion feels like the site's own interactions rather than generic video motion.

## 6. Where `flowcharts.css` translates to motion, and where it does not

**Translates directly:**

- `.flowchart-node` — `background: var(--bg-1)`, `border: 1px solid var(--border-2)`,
  **`border-top: 3px solid var(--accent)`**, `radius 4px`, `padding 20px`, internal `gap 8px`
  between a `strong` title and a `small` detail. This is the step card, unchanged. The 3px
  accent top border is the single best affordance in the stylesheet for video: it can **fill
  left to right as progress**, and go full-width accent at the moment of failure.
- `.flowchart-root::after` and the branch connectors — 1px `--border-2` vertical rules. This is
  the trail. In motion it draws rather than existing.
- `.flowchart-edge-label` — mono, 0.6875rem, weight 600, letter-spacing 0.08em, accent colour.
  Use for the transition labels between beats.
- `.flowchart-node-kicker` in `--text-3` — the small label above a step title. Use for step
  numbers.

**Does not translate, and must not be carried over:**

- **The `.flowchart` outer container** — `1px` border, `overflow: hidden`, `margin: 48px 0`. A
  video frame is already a container. Wrapping the film in a bordered card wastes frame and
  puts a static rectangle on screen for 150 seconds.
- **`.flowchart-caption`** — the `--bg-2` header strip with a title and a `small` subtitle. This
  is a title bar, and a title bar that persists is the slideshow this brief forbids. The film's
  title belongs in beat 1's motion or nowhere.
- **`grid-template-columns: repeat(2, minmax(0, 1fr))` on `.flowchart-outcomes`** — on the page
  this constrains every diagram to exactly two branches, which is a stylesheet limit rather
  than an editorial choice. **It does not bind in motion.** Beat 6 should sprout three or four
  branches and prune them, because that is what the argument needs and the page could not do.
- **`.flowchart-note`** — a trailing paragraph of caveat under a diagram. Caveats do not work in
  motion; a viewer cannot re-read them. Anything that needs a caveat goes in the transcript.

## 7. What must not be in it

- **No number the article does not contain.** The article's own "a fifteen-minute build turns
  into an hour of debugging the writer's setup" is available because the article says it. No
  percentages, no "teams that do this see X", no read-time statistics, no completion rates.
- **No invented specificity.** No named company with bad docs, no named product, no "I reviewed
  forty tutorials", no "in my experience at". Ninad has not measured this and the film must not
  imply he has.
- **No fabricated error text.** If a beat shows an error state, it shows an error *state* —
  colour, position, the trail stopping — not a fake stack trace or a made-up message from a real
  tool.
- **No claim about AI or search.** This is a documentation-craft film. Cluster isolation applies
  to video exactly as it applies to prose.
- **Diátaxis must be attributed on screen.** Presenting the four-mode split as ours would be
  taking credit for someone else's framework, on video, permanently.
- **No end-card call to action that withholds.** No "read the full article for the rest". The
  premise is that a viewer gets everything.

## 8. Placement, markup, and the transcript

**Position on page:** immediately after the two-paragraph opening, before the first `## Decide
whether this should be a tutorial`. The opening states the problem in prose, the film delivers
the whole argument, and the article body then serves the reader who wants it in text or arrived
mid-scroll. Do not put it at the top above the H1, and do not bury it below the fold.

**Schema:** a `VideoObject`, nested as `mainEntity` alongside the existing article schema rather
than replacing it. Required properties, all of which must be real:

```
name              short, matches the on-page heading
description       one sentence, distinct from the article meta description
thumbnailUrl      a real rendered frame, not a generated placeholder
uploadDate        ISO 8601, the actual publish date
duration          ISO 8601, e.g. PT2M30S — must match the rendered file
contentUrl        the MP4
embedUrl          the player, if the player differs from the file
transcript        the full text, matching the on-page transcript exactly
```

A `duration` that disagrees with the file is a lie in structured data. Read it off the render.

**Transcript: on-page, visible, not collapsed.** Under an `## What the video covers` heading
after the player, with timestamps. Not inside `<details>` — collapsed content is weaker for
extraction and the whole point is extractability.

**And yes, the transcript doubles as extractable content, deliberately.** Each timestamped line
in section 4 is written to be self-contained: "Prerequisites become checks rather than prose.
Each one either passes or it does not." stands alone as an answer to a question, with no
pronoun reaching backwards. That is the same property the glossary opening-definition rule
demands, for the same reason — an answer engine lifts a sentence out of its surroundings, and a
sentence that depends on the previous one lifts badly.

**One risk to manage:** the transcript is a compression of the article's own argument on the same
URL, so the page will carry the same claims twice in different words. That is acceptable on a
single URL — it is repetition, not duplicate content, and there is no second URL competing.
But the transcript must be the narration verbatim, not an expanded paraphrase that drifts into
being a second article.

**Egress, which Ninad raised and which is a real constraint.** Do not commit the MP4 into the
Pages build. A 2:30 render at reasonable quality is tens of megabytes, it inflates the git
history permanently, and every view is Cloudflare Pages egress. Put the file in R2 or
Cloudflare Stream and reference it. Budget the render for a hard ceiling of **20 MB** at 1080p —
this film is flat colour, hairlines and text, which encodes extremely efficiently, so that
ceiling is generous rather than tight. Ship a `poster` frame so the page costs nothing until
someone presses play.

## 9. How this gets measured

The baseline, recorded here so it cannot be revised later: **position 7.0 on `how to create tech
tutorials`, 10.8 on `technical tutorial`, 11 impressions and 0 clicks across 5 named queries in
the 90 days to 2026-08-14.**

The test is whether a video with an on-page transcript changes the click behaviour of a page
that already holds a top-10 position and earns nothing from it. Watch for a video thumbnail
appearing in the result, and for a first click.

**Stated in advance, because it is the honest part:** 11 impressions is far too thin to prove
anything. A first click would be one click. This is a format test whose result will be
suggestive at best, and the reason to run it anyway is that the alternative — publishing more
prose into a niche where 90 posts have produced zero human clicks — has already been measured
and has not worked.
